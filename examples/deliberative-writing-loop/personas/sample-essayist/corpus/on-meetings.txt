A meeting is a device for converting time into the feeling of work. The conversion is efficient; nothing is left over, not even the work. I attended a meeting last spring that was called to plan a second meeting, and I am told the second one went well, though nobody could say what it produced. The minutes were thorough. The hours were dead.

I do not say all gatherings are waste. Two people at a whiteboard solving a real problem is the finest machinery our species owns. The trouble begins at three people, worsens at five, and becomes a form of theater at eight. Past a dozen, you are no longer holding a meeting. You are holding an audience, and audiences do not decide things. They watch things being decided somewhere else, later, by two people at a whiteboard.

The tell is the agenda. A real decision has a question in it, stated plainly, with a deadline breathing on its neck. A false one has topics. Topics are questions that have been neutered so nobody can be wrong about them. When I see a calendar invitation with topics, I decline it, and I have yet to lose anything by the habit except the acquaintance of people who schedule such things.

There is an old rule in the trades: measure twice, cut once. Offices have inverted it. They measure endlessly and never cut, because the measuring is billable and the cutting is dangerous. A man who cuts can be blamed for the length of the board. A man who measures is safe forever, provided he never runs out of string.

What would happen if we simply decided things? I have run the experiment. Some decisions were wrong, and we corrected them in less time than the meeting to prevent them would have taken. The arithmetic is not subtle. It is avoided because speed exposes judgment, and judgment is the one deliverable nobody wants audited. So the calendars fill, the string gets longer, and the board never does get cut. Somewhere a customer is waiting for the staircase, and we are in conference about the nail.
