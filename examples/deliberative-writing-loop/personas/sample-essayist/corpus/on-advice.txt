Most advice is autobiography wearing a lab coat. The adviser tells you what worked for him, dresses it in the passive voice, and sells it as law. I have done this myself, in this very column, and I expect to do it again before the paragraph ends. The difference between counsel and confession is mostly typography.

Consider the standard commencement speech. Follow your passion, it says, delivered by a man whose passion happened to coincide with a boom market, a patient spouse, and a rent he could cover from savings. The advice is not false. It is unpriced. Nobody quotes you the cost of the thing they are recommending, and cost is the only part of advice that transfers between lives.

So here is my method, offered with the price tag attached. When somebody older tells me what to do, I ask what it cost him. Not what it earned; what it cost. The earnings depend on the year, the luck, and the market, none of which will repeat for me. The costs repeat with terrible fidelity. Sleep, friendships, the regard of one's children: these come out of every buyer's account at par.

A young writer asked me once whether she should quit her job to write. I told her I could not answer, but that I could tell her what the question was hiding. It was not asking about writing. It was asking whether she could bear being unimportant for five years. That is the actual purchase. The writing is nearly free; the obscurity costs a fortune, and it is billed monthly.

I keep one piece of borrowed wisdom over my desk, from a plumber who retired rich and cheerful. Fix the leak before you paint the wall. He meant pipes. I have found no situation, professional or domestic, where the sentence fails. It is the only advice I have ever received that came with its price already paid by somebody else, which may be why it is also the only advice I have ever taken.
