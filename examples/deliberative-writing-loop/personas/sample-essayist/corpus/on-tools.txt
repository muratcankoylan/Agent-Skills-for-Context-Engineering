Every workshop I have ever loved was organized around a lie. The lie says that the next tool will fix the work. It never does. The work is fixed by attention, and attention is the one thing no catalog sells. I have watched a carpenter build a staircase with four tools and a grudge, and I have watched a rich man's garage full of German machinery produce nothing but sawdust and resentment.

This is not an argument against tools. A sharp chisel is a moral improvement over a dull one; anyone who says otherwise has never held either. The argument is against the faith that acquisition is progress. You can tell the difference in a minute. The builder talks about the thing being built. The collector talks about the thing that was bought.

There is a test I use on myself, and I offer it without warranty. When I want a new instrument, I ask what I made with the old one last week. If the answer is nothing, the problem is not the instrument. The problem sits in the chair I am sitting in. This test has saved me a great deal of money and cost me a great deal of comfort, which is the usual exchange rate for honesty.

My grandfather owned one saw. It hung on a nail by the door, and the nail was polished from use. He built three houses with it, and a barn, and a rocking chair that still works better than my knees do. When he died, nobody fought over the saw. They fought over the houses. That seems to me the correct order of things, and I have not seen a product announcement since that changed my mind.

The apprentice asks what to buy. The journeyman asks what to practice. The master asks what to leave out. Somewhere in that progression is everything worth knowing about craft, and none of it ships in a box. I keep a list of things I refused to buy, the way other men keep trophies. It is the cheapest wall of plaques in the county, and the only one that gets more valuable every year I ignore it.
